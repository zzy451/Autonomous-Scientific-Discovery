Engineering Environmental sciences Image-Based Air Quality Estimation Using Deep Learning Techniques Introduction The dynamic complexity of atmospheric systems presents a fundamental challenge to urban air pollution management. The requisite synthesis of heterogeneous data and specialized domain expertise demands a level of real-time, interdisciplinary coordination that often exceeds current organizational capacities. For instance, on 26 November 2025, a large fire broke out in Hong Kong’s Tai Po district, and the ensuing 43 h combustion of building materials and foam panels across seven high-rise blocks released massive quantities of particulate matter and toxic pollutants into the local atmosphere 1 . During such acute events, local governments require precise, real-time pollution metrics and professional assessments for timely warnings and mitigation, while the general public simultaneously needs actionable advice to navigate the immediate health hazards. Comprehensively assessing these impacts, however, necessitates the integration of emission inventories, meteorological modeling, remote sensing data, chemical analyses, and health-risk assessment tools 2 , 3 , 4 . This is a highly complex procedure requiring teams of experts working for hours or days; moreover, the reliance on highly technical jargon creates a significant communication barrier for the general public, who urgently need accessible information without requiring professional expertise. These analytical workflows are challenging to orchestrate under tight timeframes, a difficulty that is acutely exacerbated in regions with limited technical expertise or monitoring infrastructure. In China, the imperative for effective management is particularly severe: an immense population suffers from PM2.5-related respiratory and cardiovascular conditions, with particulate matter contributing to over 1.42 million premature deaths annually and ranking as the fourth leading risk factor for mortality nationwide 5 , 6 . Although China maintains over 1700 national-level reference stations and thousands of provincial nodes, monitoring resources and analytical capacity remain unevenly distributed across regions 7 . This disparity creates a two-sided bottleneck in air quality management. In developed coastal agglomerations, the sheer volume and heterogeneity of monitoring data overwhelm existing expert capacity, resulting in analytical saturation. In western inland regions, by contrast, a shortage of skilled personnel and advanced analytical equipment means that even basic pollution analysis is frequently delayed, despite the availability of the underlying data 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 . Consequently, there is an urgent demand for an automated air pollution management system capable of performing complex analyses, presenting real-time data, and communicating with non-professional users. Such a system would provide critical support for both high-level decision-making and preliminary healthcare guidance, operating with less human intervention during the analytical phase, while preserving expert oversight for final policy decisions. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated emergent reasoning capabilities 12 , 13 , 14 , motivating a gradual shift from passive text-processing instruments toward increasingly autonomous scientific assistants 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 . However, the deployment of monolithic LLMs in air pollution research is fundamentally constrained. Even in expert-level environmental science and engineering question answering, GPT-4 attains an expert-rated factuality of only 0.79, dropping below 0.5 on the most difficult items 20 . This unreliability becomes critical in autonomous scientific workflows, where agentic systems succeed precisely because generated programs are filtered by external execution and evaluation rather than trusted 21 , 22 . This reliability gap widens in complex technical domains, where mainstream models frequently fail to surpass 50% accuracy in intricate calculations or domain-specific modeling, which is a limitation that fine-tuning for environmental applications has not fully resolved 20 , 23 . Furthermore, while fine-tuning embeds specific domain knowledge, it often precipitates narrow specialization and overfitting 20 , 24 , precluding the interdisciplinary breadth necessary for holistic urban air pollution analysis. More critically, LLMs possess a fundamental architectural deficit regarding complex numerical analysis 25 , 26 , 27 . These limitations are exacerbated by the challenge of integrating heterogeneous, high-frequency data streams, which frequently induces context loss and catastrophic forgetting in information-dense environments. Yet, despite their inherent lack of numerical competence, LLMs have achieved strong proficiency in code generation, enabling the synthesis of executable algorithmic scripts from natural language prompts 28 , 29 , 30 . Our proposed framework directly builds upon this capability. In this work, we introduce Neuro-Air, a multi-agent framework designed to overcome these structural limitations by automating core procedures within the urban air pollution management cycle. Rather than proposing a new retrieval or execution algorithm, the architecture integrates two capabilities from our prior systems, domain-grounded knowledge retrieval and a persistent code-execution runtime, within a hierarchical multi-agent workflow, so that the analytical pipeline is decomposed across specialized agents instead of being handled by a single monolithic model 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 . To our knowledge, this is among the first end-to-end analytical workflows of this kind assembled for air pollution analysis. Unlike prior approaches in environmental engineering that address narrowly defined analytical tasks 24 , 35 , 36 , 37 , Neuro-Air encompasses the full spectrum of the management cycle. It integrates real-time air quality data, screens emission source-receptor associations, evaluates multi-dimensional impacts, and synthesizes candidate control strategies. We evaluated the framework using a dataset of 317 complex environmental queries drawn from two contrasting application settings: acute emergency response in Hong Kong SAR, a densely monitored coastal metropolis, and chronic industrial-emission analysis in Hebei, a heavily industrialized region within the well-instrumented Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) air-quality coordination network. This validation utilized a rigorous human-in-the-loop protocol, cross-validating human expert assessments with autonomous AI Judges. To ensure methodological robustness, the framework was deployed across multiple mainstream LLM backbones to benchmark operational stability and consistency. Our results indicate that Neuro-Air mitigates several constraints of monolithic models on these tasks and provides a practical, reusable workflow for AI-assisted environmental analysis. The primary contributions of this work are threefold: Real-time assimilation of heterogeneous data: We establish an architectural protocol that integrates real-time, multimodal environmental data streams with semantic reasoning, designed to bridge the gap between raw sensor data and higher-level environmental analysis. Augmentation of LLM computational capacity: We augment foundation LLMs with algorithmic agents to mitigate their limitations in numerical reasoning and computation. This integration provides a foundation for executing complex atmospheric tasks, such as dynamic feature correlation and spatiotemporal analysis, that reside beyond the scope of monolithic models. Automation of interdisciplinary analytical workflows: We show that multi-agent collaboration can automate interdisciplinary analytical workflows that traditionally necessitate extensive human coordination, shortening the initial analytical synthesis and providing a scalable architecture to support environmental decision workflows. Ultimately, the framework serves as a decision-support tool for air quality management, with experts retaining responsibility for final judgments. It streamlines complex data integration and analytical workflows, thereby facilitating timely, data-driven regulatory action and public health protection. Results Functional overview and agent specialization A comprehensive urban air quality management lifecycle typically encompasses five core stages 38 : (1) objective identification, wherein governing institutions define policy thresholds, regulatory standards, and scientific baselines (e.g., acceptable pollutant concentrations or ozone formation mechanisms) through extensive literature and policy review; (2) pollution monitoring, which involves acquiring and processing high-resolution spatiotemporal data from sensor networks and satellite observations to establish early-warning systems and emergency analyses; (3) comprehensive assessment, requiring complex analytical procedures such as spatiotemporal distribution mapping, emission source-receptor association, meteorological factor analysis, and health risk evaluations; (4) strategy formulation, which translates complex analytical outputs into actionable mitigation strategies and accessible reports for non-expert stakeholders; and (5) continuous evaluation, ensuring ongoing monitoring of policy implementation and efficacy. To deploy LLMs across these interdisciplinary workflows, we built a suite of five domain-specific agents, structured into three functional modules: (i) External Knowledge and Data Extraction Agents (data pre-processing and knowledge retrieval); (ii) Analytical Agents (air pollution modeling and multi-variate analysis); and (iii) Synthesis and Communication Agents (visualization and report generation), as illustrated in Fig. 1 a. We used Claude 4 Sonnet 39 as the baseline backbone and benchmarked additional models (GPT-5 40 , Gemini 2.5 Pro 41 , DeepSeek-V3 42 ) in the evaluation below; each agent was initialized with a role-specific system prompt (shown in Table 2 ). A central LLM Coordinator interprets each query and decomposes it into step-wise subtasks using Chain-of-Thought 43 reasoning and Retrieval-Augmented Generation 44 , 45 , then orchestrates the specialized agents, each running under a role-defining prompt over the dual-stream runtime described below (configurations in Methods). In the final synthesis stage, the Coordinator consolidates the agents’ outputs into a coherent response, which may comprise textual analyses, quantitative summaries, visualizations, or policy recommendations. This work builds upon two of our previous studies. The first is AirGPT 46 , a domain-specific framework that integrates conversational AI with atmospheric science expertise. By drawing on a curated corpus of peer-reviewed literature and specialized data analysis tools, AirGPT addresses the outdated and fabricated information that limits general-purpose LLMs in air quality management. Figure 2 illustrates the complete external information and data retrieval process introduced in that work. The second is CaveAgent 47 , which employs a dual-stream architecture in which a persistent Python runtime, rather than the text context, serves as the central locus of state. By using the LLM as a runtime operator, this design enables the system to handle the complex computational problems. While AirGPT established the informational foundation (curated domain knowledge and external data pipelines) and CaveAgent provided the computational substrate (stateful Python runtime for numerical execution), neither system alone addresses the full complexity of urban air-pollution governance. AirGPT lacks autonomous computational capacity, as it retrieves and synthesizes information but cannot independently execute quantitative analyses. CaveAgent, conversely, provides execution capabilities but operates as a general-purpose runtime without domain-specialized analytical reasoning or multi-source data integration protocols. Neuro-Air’s contribution is the architectural integration of these complementary capabilities within a hierarchical multi-agent framework: it couples domain-grounded knowledge retrieval with autonomous computational execution, orchestrated by specialized agents that collectively span the entire pollution-management lifecycle. Within minutes of the event, the isolated execution environment flagged acute short-term anomalies at the nearest monitoring station during the first 24 h: peak PM 2.5 reached 54.6 μ g/m 3 (2.0 × the pre-fire mean) and peak NO 2 reached 123.2 μ g/m 3 (2.5 × the pre-fire mean). We emphasize that this output should be interpreted as a rapid, data-grounded screening signal that helps prioritize expert attention during an unfolding emergency, not as evidence of causation. The peak-to-mean ratio indicates the prominence of a short-term excursion but cannot, on its own, establish its cause, as similar peaks may arise from ordinary diurnal variability. The agent further cross-referenced the meteorological data and correctly noted that, despite the acute 24 h excursion, multi-day average pollution levels showed no sustained increase. Indeed, the station’s official AQHI remained within the moderate range (5–6) throughout the event. The system-generated meteorological summary reported a modal westerly wind component and a mean wind speed of 8.5 m/s based on 24 h aggregates. These results faithfully reflect the underlying database records; however, the westerly mode is driven largely by post-event observations, making the 24 h aggregation too coarse for event-specific interpretation. We therefore provide an hour-resolved expert reanalysis below while retaining the original system output verbatim in Supplementary Note 3 , together with an editorial note. Because the screening signal alone does not support causal attribution, we conducted the follow-up analyses specified by our governance framework: comparison against a matched historical baseline, a before-and-after network contrast using a difference-in-differences framework, and an interrupted-time-series-style analysis of the event-aligned hourly observations. First, a historical matched-hour baseline (the same station and clock hour over the preceding 14 days) shows the fire-evening NO 2 value (123.2 μ g/m 3 at 20:00) to be high but not unprecedented ( z = 1.9; the matched-hour maximum of the preceding fortnight was 125.5 μ g/m 3 on a non-fire evening), confirming that the raw peak-to-mean ratio overstates the anomaly. Second, a network contrast across all 18 general and roadside stations, in which the nearest station’s change against its own matched-hour baseline is differenced against the contemporaneous changes at control stations (a difference-in-differences reading), reveals that the same evening exhibited a territory-wide NO 2 elevation (mean rise of + 19.6 μ g/m 3 against matched hours; several stations far upwind of the fire rose as much as, or more than, the nearest station), coincident with hour-resolved observations of the northeast-monsoon flow weakening to near calm in the sheltered Tolo basin during the evening hours–conditions favouring general pollutant accumulation rather than a single-source plume. Third, the pollutant contrast most consistent with a local combustion contribution tells the most defensible story: in the evening network contrast the nearest station ranked first of 18 in PM 2.5 elevation ( + 7.4 μ g/m 3 above the network mean rise), a modest but direction-consistent local excess. The resulting synthesis is appropriately qualified yet operationally useful. The expert reanalysis indicates that the fire’s discernible local signature was a moderate elevation in particulate matter superimposed on a broader regional stagnation episode. The system’s minutes-scale screening appropriately prompted further investigation, whereas final attribution depended on expert-level reanalysis. This delineation of responsibilities reflects the human-AI division of labour underlying our framework. As part of the real-time workflow, the system complemented pollutant screening with a proximity-based indicator of potential exposure. It generated four concentric distance bands (0–500, 500–1000, 1000–1500, and 1500–2000 m) around the incident location and identified 293 residential buildings within 1.5 km, as well as 1090 buildings of all types within 2 km. The scope of interpretation, however, should be clearly delineated. In the absence of plume-direction, occupancy, and population data, these building counts indicate spatial proximity to the incident but do not quantify population exposure. Nevertheless, the analysis identified the Tai Po Baptist Public School, located approximately 35 m from the incident location, as a potentially sensitive receptor warranting priority expert review and consideration of targeted health communication. The synthesis and communication agent subsequently integrated these analytical outputs and generated visual summaries of the spatial distribution and temporal evolution of pollutant concentrations (Fig. 3 b–d). It then used the LLM’s semantic reasoning capabilities to translate the quantitative results into an accessible natural-language report for non-specialist stakeholders. A complete workflow and the generated answer can be found in Supplementary Note 3 . For emergency monitoring and early warning, the workflow provides two practical advantages over standalone LLMs. First, deterministic pipelines retrieve hourly observations directly from sensor networks, enabling near-real-time analysis without relying exclusively on the model’s static training knowledge. Second, the system grounds its analytical outputs in retrieved data and reproducible computations. Its visualizations, recommendations, and advisories are therefore traceable to the underlying evidence and analytical procedures, allowing experts to inspect and verify each stage of the analytical process. Multi-variate emission analysis Beyond rapid emergency response, environmental management requires multi-variate analysis of the non-linear interactions among meteorology, traffic, and static geographical features such as topography and land use. To demonstrate this, we present an exploratory source-receptor association and emission-concentration screening case study of an iron smelting facility in Handan, Hebei (Fig. 4 ). Handan is a heavily industrialized priority city within the BTH air-quality coordination network. Its local analytical capacity, however, remains constrained by limited specialized expertise and advanced instrumentation, such that even routine air-quality assessments can require considerable time and manual effort. This setting complements the acute-event Hong Kong case by demonstrating the potential value of the framework in contexts with limited analytical resources. Full size image a Task initialization identifying factory coordinates and emission logs; ( b ) Geofenced screening of candidate receptor stations around the facility; ( c ) Python-based time-series analysis comparing emission fluctuations; ( d ) Evaluation of regulatory compliance, emission-concentration associations, and related metrics; ( e ) Synthesis of findings and automated report generation. In this scenario, Neuro-Air is tasked with analyzing carbon monoxide (CO) emissions from a large iron and steel facility in She County, Handan, Hebei, and evaluating their localized impact on ambient air quality from January 6 to 8, 2026 (Fig. 4 a). Upon receiving the query, the Central Coordinator orchestrates the analytical pipeline. Initially, the knowledge retrieval and data acquisition agents identify the facility’s spatial coordinates and extract the corresponding high-resolution emission logs and operational data. To identify which nearby monitoring stations are most strongly associated with the facility’s emissions, Neuro-Air autonomously geofences candidate receptor stations around the source and screens them by emission-concentration cross-correlation, rather than a physically calibrated dispersion model. By setting a 20 km geofencing radius, the agent retrieves synchronized ambient monitoring data from 18 surrounding stations. It then generates native Python code leveraging the SciPy library to compute the cross-correlation between the facility’s emission vectors and the ambient concentration signals (Fig. 4 b). Through this isolated execution, the system ranks the candidate receptors by association strength, identifying the Guxin Air Quality Station (located 12.2 km to the south of the facility) as the most strongly associated node, with a strong baseline correlation ( r = 0.77, p < 0.01). Algorithm 2 Autonomous source-receptor Association and Time-Lag Correlation To characterize the temporal association between facility emissions and ambient pollutant concentrations, the system calculated Pearson correlations over candidate lags of τ ∈ {0, …, 6} h. At Guxin, the maximum correlation occurred when concentrations were aligned with emissions recorded three hours earlier ( r = 0.83; Fig. 4 c). We interpret this as an empirical lag of maximum correlation, not a physically resolved transport time. Following Algorithm 2, the system then defined high- and low-emission periods using the 67th and 33rd percentiles of the emission distribution ( > 14, 288 and < 11, 857 kg/h, respectively). Under contemporaneous alignment, mean CO at Guxin was approximately 130% higher during high-emission hours than during low-emission hours (0.78 versus 0.34 mg/m 3 ), with a corresponding 16-point difference in mean AQI (Fig. 4 d). Because this comparison did not incorporate the identified three-hour lag, we repeated the analysis using lag-aligned observations. The association remained similar or slightly stronger: under the original thresholds, the difference in mean CO between high- and low-emission periods was + 0.46 mg/m 3 ( + 139%), accompanied by an 18-point difference in mean AQI. After recalculating the thresholds using the 69 lag-aligned pairs, the relative difference was + 134%. A moving-block bootstrap using 12-hour blocks and B = 10 4 replicates, with thresholds re-estimated in each replicate, yielded 95% intervals that excluded zero under both contemporaneous alignment ( + 0.20 to + 0.59 mg/m 3 ) and three-hour lag alignment ( + 0.24 to + 0.57 mg/m 3 ). The intervals remained bounded away from zero for block lengths of 6–24 h, indicating that the original difference between emission periods was not an artifact of temporal alignment. Nevertheless, these results represent statistical associations rather than causal effects and remain subject to the confounding factors discussed below. The system also generated targeted visualizations and a management-oriented synthesis (Fig. 4 e); the complete workflow and original response are provided in Supplementary Note 4 . Algorithm 2 summarizes the lag-consistent screening procedure used by Neuro-Air: it retrieves facility emission records and concentration measurements from nearby monitoring stations, evaluates candidate lags, identifies the lag of maximum correlation, retains associations that meet the predefined screening criterion, and compares concentrations between high- and low-emission periods under the selected temporal alignment. This analysis supports a statistical association between facility emissions and concentrations measured at Guxin, not a physically validated attribution of the observed pollution to the facility. Because the system’s meteorological query returned no wind records for the analysis window, its directional labels reflected only the geometric relationship between the facility and monitoring station; the wind field was therefore reconstructed independently by experts. Predominantly southwesterly winds prevailed and generally did not support direct transport from the facility to Guxin, which is located to its south. Shared diurnal cycles, regional background pollution, or emissions from other nearby facilities may therefore partly explain the observed correlation. Robust attribution is further limited by the short record ( ~ 72 hourly observations), temporal autocorrelation, shared meteorological forcing, and selection of the maximum correlation across 18 monitoring stations and 7 candidate lags. The r > 0.5 and p < 0.01 thresholds in Algorithm 2 should therefore be treated as descriptive screening criteria rather than formal inferential tests, particularly because the original procedure did not correct for the 126 comparisons arising from all combinations of stations and lags. As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, a Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all 126 comparisons retained the association between Guxin concentrations and facility emissions at a three-hour lag at a nominal false-discovery rate of 0.01 (unadjusted p = 7.3 × 10 −19 ). This correction, however, does not account for temporal dependence or environmental confounding. Moreover, 48 combinations involving 9 of the 18 monitoring stations met the original screening criteria, indicating a spatially diffuse pattern of correlations. This pattern is consistent with shared diurnal variation and regional background pollution rather than an association uniquely localized to Guxin. An independent companion analysis likewise did not support a physically credible transport relationship: the correlation was higher under winds unfavourable for transport from the facility to Guxin ( r = 0.777, n = 62) than under favourable winds ( r = 0.640, n = 8), although the small number of favourable-wind observations limits this comparison. The inconsistent physical interpretations obtained from the same data demonstrate that reliable code execution does not ensure stable source attribution and underscore the need for expert review. Regulatory use would require wind-resolved dispersion analysis, correction for multiple comparisons across monitoring stations and candidate lags, explicit treatment of temporal dependence and confounding, and validation using independent observations. A key factor underlying Neuro-Air’s effectiveness in this workflow is its dual-stream architecture, which decouples semantic reasoning from mathematical computation. General-purpose LLMs may produce unsupported outputs in pollution source attribution, such as selecting monitoring stations without sufficient scientific justification, and such limitations cannot be fully resolved by simply embedding physical equations in the prompt. Conversely, task-specific fine-tuned models may lack the flexibility required for changing analytical conditions. Neuro-Air addresses these limitations by using the LLM for logical orchestration and code generation while delegating numerical modeling to an isolated Python environment. This separation improves methodological transparency by allowing researchers to inspect generated scripts, intermediate outputs, and data logs for analytical validity and data provenance. For complex atmospheric analyses involving iterative and multi-step calculations, direct Python execution can also reduce repeated model calls and external tool interactions, thereby lowering latency, computational overhead, and token consumption. By transforming heterogeneous sensor data into actionable information, Neuro-Air bridges raw environmental observations and downstream decision-making, providing preliminary guidance on pollution conditions and associated health considerations. Evaluation of Neuro-Air’s performance We assembled a multi-source dataset (air quality, traffic, meteorological, geospatial, and emission data) for Hong Kong 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 and Hebei Province, and curated a benchmark of 317 representative queries (200 Hebei, 117 Hong Kong) drawn from practitioner interviews, official government documentation, and citizen inquiries 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 ; the two queries driving the case studies are held out and reported separately. The queries are stratified into six functional domains (Temporal Pattern, Geospatial, Multivariate Environmental, Urban Environmental Planning, Environmental Decision Support, and Personal Environmental Action Planning), collectively spanning spatio-temporal analysis, city-level planning, and management guidance. Full metadata and the benchmark inventory are in Supplementary Note 1 (Data Acquisition and Benchmark Construction), which lists the query inventories for Hebei and Hong Kong (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 ) alongside the corresponding data sources and characteristics (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 ). To rigorously assess Neuro-Air and mitigate hallucinations across the entire analytical lifecycle, we score every response against five core criteria: (1) Data Integration and Synthesis (DIS), which assesses the absolute accuracy of empirical data retrieval; (2) Methodological Soundness (MS), which evaluates the contextual appropriateness of the selected analytical models; (3) Technical Implementation Quality (TIQ), which verifies the executable validity of the generated programmatic code; (4) Response Alignment (RA), which measures the comprehensive fulfillment of the user’s initial query; and (5) Faithfulness (FAI), which ensures that all final conclusions are strictly derived from transparent, intermediate computational evidence. The comprehensive performance score is the average of these five criteria. Detailed operational definitions and step-by-step verification protocols are provided in Supplementary Note 2 (Detailed Evaluation Framework and Protocols); the overall 1–10 scoring scale is given in Supplementary Table 5 , and the complete scoring rubric applied by the human experts is provided in Supplementary Note 5 , with the full scale as administered to the panel in Supplementary Table 11 . We apply these criteria through four complementary channels 46 , 59 , 60 , organized around two levels of quality. Execution-level quality, namely data retrieval, computation, and code execution, is captured by the three automated channels; professional-level quality, namely whether a response is dependable enough to inform a real-world air-quality management decision and thus requires environmental judgment that execution accuracy alone cannot capture, is probed by the human panel. (i) A multiple AI-judge evaluation scores all 317 queries on the five criteria using three independent LLM judges (Kimi-K2, Claude 4 Sonnet, Qwen3), each judge scoring every response once, with the final score taken as the mean across the three judges, across the four foundation-model backbones. (ii) A judge-free objective channel grades 90 programmatically verified tasks against ground truth recomputed live from the database (this benchmark is detailed below with the ablation). (iii) Objective process metrics are parsed deterministically from the complete execution logs. (iv) A blinded human calibration study was conducted with 15 active environmental-management professionals, including atmospheric analysts, data engineers, and policymakers. Each evaluator independently scored the responses generated by the baseline Claude 4 Sonnet backbone under the full multi-agent configuration using a holistic 1–10 rubric. Evaluation was performed on a difficulty-weighted subset of 32 queries (11 from Hong Kong and 21 from Hebei) covering all six task categories. The subset was selected by stratified random sampling across task categories, followed by manual review to retain more challenging items. In the fully crossed design, evaluators could inspect the generated code and intermediate execution outputs alongside the final answer, but were blinded to the automated scores; disjoint calibration examples were provided for reference. Because the worker roles form sequentially dependent stages of the workflow, removing an individual worker would disrupt the pipeline rather than provide a meaningful partial ablation; we therefore use the fully merged configuration as the comparison. For the execution-paradigm ablation, we compare native Python execution with a conventional function-calling workflow. Function calling requires repeated exchanges between the model and external tools during iterative analyses, whereas Neuro-Air generates the analytical procedure as executable Python code and runs it within an isolated sandbox. This reduces repeated communication and token use while keeping numerical operations within a reproducible execution environment. The computational cost of the architecture remains measurable and interpretable: the multi-agent configuration consumed 3.3–4.4 × the median tokens per task (Fig. 6 d and Supplementary Table 6 ). This additional cost primarily reflects the decomposition and independent auditing introduced by the multi-agent design, rather than greater use of the available execution budget. The single-agent configuration generally terminated well within the same budget, while many of its failures arose from semantic errors that were not identified through additional computation alone. These automated and architectural results, however, capture only execution-level competence. We therefore conducted a blinded expert-panel evaluation to provide a more demanding assessment of professional quality. Automated judges consistently assigned higher scores than the experts, with a mean difference of + 0.24 on the 0–1 scale across task types (Fig. 6 b). Agreement among individual experts was modest (ICC(2,1) = 0.38), whereas the mean rating across all 15 experts, used as the human reference, showed high reliability (ICC(2,15) = 0.90). This individual-level disagreement highlights the inherent subjectivity of professional judgments on complex analytical outputs. The systematic score gap suggests that experts applied a stricter standard of professional adequacy. This was particularly evident for health-impact questions and source-attribution tasks. For the latter, professional assessment often requires domain-grounded emission characterization and dispersion modeling, whereas Neuro-Air typically relied on proximity- or correlation-based reasoning. These results illustrate that correct execution does not necessarily translate into professionally adequate analysis. Full agreement and inter-rater reliability statistics are reported in Supplementary Note 2 . The gap was driven by a recurring set of failure modes that became more frequent as task complexity increased. On questions requiring source-receptor or transport reasoning, the system frequently substituted same-hour concentration correlation or simple proximity for genuine dispersion or trajectory analysis, omitting wind-direction gating, transport lag, and plume decay, and in some cases identified a facility that was in fact downwind as a dominant contributor. Temporal and causal mismatches arose when long-term or multi-day averages, combined with small-sample correlations, were used to answer questions about specific dates or causal attribution. The system also did not consistently validate query premises against the retrieved data: it sometimes accepted a stated “strong wind” when measurements indicated light winds, or a “severe PM 2.5 alert” when the exceedance concerned PM 10 and no alert was in effect, and some failed wind, gradient, or direction queries were dropped silently rather than surfaced. These failures concentrated in the more complex source-receptor, spatial-exposure, and health-assessment tasks, whereas straightforward temporal-comparison queries were handled most reliably; this gradient mirrors the objective difficulty pattern reported above and delimits where the current system can and cannot be trusted. In summary, the four evaluation channels converge on a consistent but carefully bounded picture. The automated multi-judge protocol and objective process metrics indicate that Neuro-Air’s execution core is highly reliable, with aggregate scores above 85%. The controlled ablation further shows that the complete multi-agent pipeline improves programmatically verified accuracy over a merged single-agent baseline across all tested backbones, with a ~ 3B-active-parameter backbone in the multi-agent configuration reaching the accuracy range of a much larger backbone operated as a single agent. The blinded expert evaluation, however, identifies important limits to autonomous use. Performance remains less dependable for source-attribution, spatial-exposure, and health-assessment tasks that require transport modeling, premise validation, or domain-specific professional judgment. More importantly, the health-related outputs produced by Neuro-Air are not based on clinical diagnosis or individualized medical assessment and therefore should not be treated as validated health advice or communicated directly to the public without appropriate professional review. These findings distinguish reliable computational execution from professional adequacy. Neuro-Air can rapidly integrate and analyze heterogeneous environmental data, but its outputs, particularly those involving health implications, should be regarded as decision-support information rather than a substitute for expert environmental, public-health, or clinical judgment. Discussion In this study, we introduce Neuro-Air, an autonomous, multi-agent framework engineered to serve as an end-to-end analytical assistant for urban air quality management. By automating the analytical lifecycle, the system performs fundamental air pollution management tasks, such as acute emergency early-warning and exploratory source-receptor association, and synthesizes non-expert visualizations for public communication. The architectural core is a dual-stream design, inherited from our CaveAgent runtime 47 , that decouples semantic reasoning from mathematical execution; the contribution here is its integration with domain-grounded retrieval under a hierarchical multi-agent workflow, which delivers auditable performance across multiple LLM backbones and task domains. Neuro-Air can therefore substantially shorten the initial analytical synthesis that traditionally required extended inter-institutional coordination, while incurring bounded, transparently reported computational cost and preserving the capacity for subsequent expert review. A matched ablation shows that the complete multi-agent pipeline outperforms a merged single-agent baseline under the same execution-step cap, albeit at 3.3–4.4 × the token cost, positioning Neuro-Air as a capability equalizer that delivers accurate quantitative performance from smaller, lower-cost backbones in resource-constrained governance settings. Crucially, this design also mitigates the transparency issues typically associated with LLMs: because the workflow generates explicit, readable code and data logs, environmental engineers and policymakers can directly audit the system’s reasoning, data provenance, and execution logic. Compared with previous domain-specific LLM applications, Neuro-Air is firmly anchored to the empirical realities of acute pollution management, prioritizing functional execution and computational accuracy over generalized text generation. The scope of this evidence is nonetheless bounded. Our results support Neuro-Air as a reusable analytical workflow rather than a single tuned demonstration; however, both study regions are data-rich settings with structured, well-maintained databases, and performance in data-scarce or differently structured environments remains untested. We therefore frame the contribution as a transferable methodology that can be re-instantiated for new regions and tasks, while emphasizing that broader external validation is required before strong claims of general applicability can be made. Within these bounds, we further characterized the system’s failure modes through blinded expert review of the complete responses, including the generated code and intermediate outputs. Although the system addresses straightforward temporal-comparison queries with high reliability, it still lacks the ability to perform certain operations required by complex tasks such as source-receptor or spatial-exposure problems, including wind-gated, transport-lagged reasoning, correct geospatial handling, and premise validation. For health assessment, another limitation applies: the system’s outputs are not based on clinical diagnosis or calibrated against professional medical or public-health standards, and should therefore be treated only as preliminary reference information rather than validated health advice. In these cases, expert review is essential to ensure that the conclusions drawn from the system’s analysis are valid and actionable. Overall, while Neuro-Air represents an advancement in AI-assisted environmental decision support, its limitations and the continued need for human oversight in complex environmental analyses must be recognized. Taken together, these results define the role of Neuro-Air more clearly. Under data-rich conditions, the system provides a dependable computational core for air-quality analysis. It can retrieve and integrate heterogeneous environmental data, including emission, meteorological, and geospatial information, and perform accurate, reproducible, and auditable code-based analyses. However, it is not yet a fully autonomous decision-making system for complex environmental questions. Neuro-Air does not perform mechanistic dispersion or trajectory modeling, nor does it provide regulatory-grade source apportionment. Its quantitative outputs are therefore better interpreted as associational and exploratory rather than causal. This distinction is particularly important for source-attribution, proximity-based exposure estimation, and analyses based on short observation windows, incomplete wind information, or potentially confounded correlations. In such settings, the system can identify patterns and generate screening signals for further investigation, but reliability decreases as task complexity, data limitations, and the need for transport reasoning increase. These limitations become more consequential when the outputs could inform policy or health-related decisions. Neuro-Air should not be used autonomously for professional-grade attribution, healthcare recommendations, or major emission-management decisions. In these settings, its outputs should serve as preliminary evidence for expert review rather than as a basis for direct action. Accordingly, the main value of Neuro-Air lies in streamlining the early stages of environmental analysis. It organizes relevant data, performs reproducible computation, and presents initial patterns and statistics in an accessible form, providing a foundation for more detailed and context-specific professional assessment. Building on these findings, a central near-term goal is the development of a more comprehensive air quality management benchmark that enables real-life analysis. Bridging LLM-based generative reasoning with deterministic atmospheric physical modeling remains a substantial challenge, and we intend to pursue directions that combine LLMs with expert environmental knowledge, such as mechanistic dispersion modeling, and potentially with established numerical models such as GEOS-Chem, WRF-Chem, and CMAQ. We hope that this exploration will establish a foundation for AI-assisted decision support in urban environmental management. Functioning agents Neuro-Air integrates a suite of specialized environmental functional agents that collectively encompass a comprehensive range of analytical capabilities. These agents are systematically designed to address the multifaceted demands of urban air-pollution assessment and environmental data analytics. Functionally, they constitute the foundational blueprint that governs how the LLM interacts with external data sources, computational tools, and user instructions within an environmental-science context. By explicitly defining the agent’s identity, technical environment, operational workflow, and behavioral boundaries, Neuro-Air moves beyond the conventional role of conversational systems and operates as an analytical framework capable of querying structured databases, executing Python-based computations, and dynamically generating interpretable visualizations. Through detailed specifications of its functional components, such as the integrated database engine for real-time air-quality queries and the ECharts configuration manager for interactive data visualization, the system prompt bridges natural-language reasoning with scientific-grade computation. This structured architecture ensures that Neuro-Air’s responses remain contextually precise, methodologically rigorous, and empirically verifiable. The internal logic of Neuro-Air’s architecture is organized around three interdependent layers: (i) a role-definition layer, in which Neuro-Air is conceptualized as a multidisciplinary environmental intelligence framework capable of interpreting, analyzing, and visualizing complex air-pollution phenomena. It merges the interpretative flexibility of large language models with the procedural rigor of environmental informatics and data science. (ii) a task-execution layer, whereby the system prompt formalizes the entire analytical workflow by which natural-language queries are transformed into executable procedures. This encompasses structured data retrieval through SQL, computational reasoning in Python, spatiotemporal aggregation, and visualization synthesis. Each process follows a precisely defined procedural syntax, ensuring analytical reproducibility and cross-session consistency; and (iii) a constraint-and-compliance layer: to safeguard analytical integrity, the framework enforces strict methodological constraints: Neuro-Air is restricted to tasks relevant to the air-quality database, must seek clarification when confronted with ambiguous queries, and is required to produce concise Python code encapsulated within a standardized <think> + code block format. External tools or unverified resources are disallowed. This constraint-driven design is intended to support transparency, accuracy, and auditability across all analyses. Collectively, these elements constitute a coherent cognitive and operational framework that establishes Neuro-Air as a new class of LLM-driven scientific reasoning systems, bridging natural-language interaction with quantitative environmental intelligence. Matched single-agent versus multi-agent ablation To evaluate the contribution of the multi-agent architecture independently of subjective judging, we constructed a 90-task programmatically verified (PV) subset from the 317-query benchmark (Fig. 5 ). The subset comprised 78 Hebei and 12 Hong Kong queries and covered five task categories: spatial analysis (27), data retrieval (20), comparative assessment (18), statistical characterization (16), and temporal pattern analysis (9). Tasks were eligible only when the expected output could be expressed as one or more uniquely determined numeric or textual values that were recomputable from the source databases. Open-ended synthesis, qualitative ranking, and policy or action-planning tasks were therefore excluded. The subset was curated before model evaluation and frozen for use across all experimental conditions. Ground-truth values were generated using human-reviewed SQL and Python reference implementations. At scoring time, each validator re-executed the corresponding reference computation against the source database and compared the result with typed variables produced by the agent. Numeric outputs were assessed using predefined task-specific tolerances, whereas counts and identifiers required exact matches. The validator set was frozen before the experimental campaign, hidden from the agents, and applied identically to both architectures. Final scoring was performed against an idle database to avoid database contention being misclassified as task failure. Scoring was therefore deterministic and involved neither human judgment nor an LLM judge. The single-agent condition merged all specialized roles into one agent with access to the complete toolchain. The multi-agent condition used the Central Coordinator, five specialized worker agents, and the in-loop computational auditing stage, with all agents sharing a persistent Python runtime. For each backbone, the two conditions used the same database, runtime environment, sampling temperature, and a byte-identical system prompt verified by content fingerprint. Both conditions were limited to 20 execution steps and a single attempt per task; in the multi-agent condition, the 20-step budget was shared across agents. This constraint equalized the permitted number of execution steps rather than token consumption, with token costs reported separately in Supplementary Table 6 . No backbone-specific prompt tuning was performed. Five backbones spanning different model families and capability levels were evaluated: DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GPT-5.5, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen2.5-72B, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B. Sampling temperature was held identical between architectural conditions for each backbone, subject to provider API constraints. Because provider APIs did not expose sampling seeds, run-level reproducibility was bounded by the fixed sampling configuration, documented model snapshots and access windows, and the single-attempt protocol. All completed runs were archived in full. Provider-side endpoint failures were defined as runs recording zero execution steps, zero tokens, and an empty variable set. These infrastructure failures were excluded symmetrically and re-run until each architectural condition contained 90 genuinely executed tasks per backbone. Architecture effects were then assessed using exact two-sided McNemar tests on paired task outcomes for each backbone and across the 450 pooled task-backbone pairs. Pooled accuracy estimates are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Because the same 90 tasks were reused across all five backbones, we additionally performed a task-level cluster bootstrap in which tasks were resampled with replacement while retaining their five paired backbone observations ( B = 100, 000, seed 20260709). Full statistical specifications, exclusion criteria, and execution-cost metrics are reported in Supplementary Table 6 . Execution-paradigm comparison To compare execution paradigms, we evaluated a function-calling baseline in which the agent accessed the same Hong Kong databases through discrete tool calls, with tool outputs returned to the model as structured messages. This differs from Neuro-Air’s native-execution approach, in which analytical logic is generated as code and executed within a persistent Python runtime. The comparison included 117 function-calling runs collected in August 2025 and 702 Hong Kong runs from the six backbones evaluated under native execution. Logs from both settings were analyzed using the same rule-based parser and metric definitions, including first-attempt execution success, the proportion of runs containing execution errors, and the distribution of errors at the SQL or tool interface. Because the function-calling baseline predates the standardized experimental protocol and its backbone identity was not retained, this comparison is not a fully matched factorial experiment. We therefore use it only to characterize execution-level differences between the two paradigms and do not draw backbone-specific conclusions. Technical design Neuro-Air supports urban air-quality analyses across a range of complexity levels. For clarity, we group these tasks into three broad categories: basic queries involving information retrieval and descriptive statistics; intermediate queries requiring data aggregation, feature extraction, visualization, or multivariate analysis; and advanced queries involving multi-stage environmental assessment and decision support. These categories are descriptive rather than rigid, as a single workflow may involve operations from multiple levels. For basic retrieval tasks, the system first interprets the user query q and evaluates whether the requested information is supported by the available data sources. Let \({\mathcal{D}}\) denote the available database schema. A relevance function \(R(q,{\mathcal{D}})\) determines whether the query is sufficiently aligned with the available data: $$R(q,{\mathcal{D}})={\mathbb{I}}\,\left[{\rm{sim}}(q,{\mathcal{D}})\ge \tau \right],$$ (1) where \({\rm{sim}}(\cdot ,\cdot )\) denotes semantic similarity and θ is the relevance threshold ( θ = 0.5 in our implementation). Queries that do not satisfy this criterion are halted or returned for clarification rather than being processed under unsupported assumptions. When external contextual information is required, the knowledge-retrieval component \({\mathcal{K}}\) retrieves relevant information from approved external sources. For intermediate and advanced analyses, Neuro-Air uses a dual-stream architecture that separates semantic reasoning from numerical execution. At interaction step t , the system maintains a semantic state $${H}_{t}={h}_{t},{o}_{t},$$ (2) where h t contains the reasoning history and o t contains observations returned by the execution environment, together with a persistent runtime state $${S}_{t}={N}_{t},{R}_{t},$$ (3) where N t contains active variables, functions, models, and data objects, and R t contains intermediate computational results. The Semantic Stream generates computational instructions from the current query and semantic context: $${c}_{t}={f}_{\theta }({h}_{t},{q}_{t}),$$ (4) where f θ denotes the underlying language model. The Runtime Stream executes these instructions within the persistent Python environment: $${S}_{t+1}={\Phi }({S}_{t},{c}_{t}),$$ (5) where Φ( ⋅ ) represents the runtime execution operator. The persistent namespace retains previously generated computational objects across successive steps, $${N}_{t+1}\supseteq {N}_{t},$$ (6) allowing multi-step analyses to proceed without repeatedly reconstructing intermediate data or computational state. Complex workflows are decomposed into an ordered sequence of analytical tasks, $${\mathcal{W}}={T}_{1},{T}_{2},\ldots ,{T}_{n},$$ (7) which may include data preprocessing, statistical analysis, geospatial processing, visualization, and environmental assessment. Each stage operates on the outputs of preceding stages: $${R}_{i+1}={{\Phi }}_{i}({R}_{i},{c}_{i}),$$ (8) where Φ i ( ⋅ ) denotes the execution operator associated with task T i . The resulting intermediate outputs are subsequently integrated into a structured analytical response containing relevant quantitative results, visualizations, and contextual interpretation. This separation between semantic orchestration and persistent numerical execution allows Neuro-Air to maintain computational continuity across multi-step workflows while keeping the generated analytical procedures inspectable and reproducible. Operational safety and reliability Because Neuro-Air is intended for decision-support applications, its execution environment incorporates safeguards for data integrity, code execution, untrusted input, and failure containment. All agents access the environmental databases, including sensor networks, emission inventories, and meteorological records, through read-only connections. The system therefore cannot insert, modify, or delete source records during analysis. Generated Python and SQL code is executed within an isolated runtime separated from the semantic reasoning process (see Technical Design). Before execution, a static security checker inherited from the CaveAgent runtime 47 screens each code block for prohibited operations. These include database-mutating statements such as UPDATE , DROP , and DELETE ; interpreter-escape or process-control operations; disallowed imports; and other non-whitelisted system operations. Code that fails these checks is rejected before execution. Neuro-Air also separates trusted system and role instructions from untrusted user input and externally retrieved content. Retrieved text is treated as data rather than executable instruction, while relevance filtering limits the external information admitted into the analytical workflow. This separation is intended to reduce the risk of prompt injection or instruction redirection from untrusted content. A dedicated adversarial security evaluation was not conducted in this study. To constrain resource use and prevent uncontrolled execution, each query is subject to a fixed execution-step budget, bounded retry and auto-correction attempts, and predefined limits on spatial and temporal query scope. These constraints prevent unbounded execution loops and support reproducible evaluation of first-attempt code validity. For auditability, the system records the generated SQL and Python code, execution outputs, intermediate variables, and retrieved inputs for each task. Quantitative claims in the final response can therefore be compared against the corresponding computational traces, forming the basis of the Faithfulness evaluation described above. When required data are unavailable or a query contains an invalid premise, the system is designed to halt execution, request clarification, or return the limitation rather than infer unsupported values. These safeguards support the use of Neuro-Air as an analytical decision-support system, while professional review remains necessary before its outputs are used for consequential policy, operational, or health-related decisions. The requisite synthesis of heterogeneous data and specialized domain expertise demands a level of real-time, interdisciplinary coordination that often exceeds current organizational capacities. For instance, on 26 November 2025, a large fire broke out in Hong Kong’s Tai Po district, and the ensuing 43 h combustion of building materials and foam panels across seven high-rise blocks released massive quantities of particulate matter and toxic pollutants into the local atmosphere 1 . During such acute events, local governments require precise, real-time pollution metrics and professional assessments for timely warnings and mitigation, while the general public simultaneously needs actionable advice to navigate the immediate health hazards. Comprehensively assessing these impacts, however, necessitates the integration of emission inventories, meteorological modeling, remote sensing data, chemical analyses, and health-risk assessment tools 2 , 3 , 4 . This is a highly complex procedure requiring teams of experts working for hours or days; moreover, the reliance on highly technical jargon creates a significant communication barrier for the general public, who urgently need accessible information without requiring professional expertise. These analytical workflows are challenging to orchestrate under tight timeframes, a difficulty that is acutely exacerbated in regions with limited technical expertise or monitoring infrastructure. In China, the imperative for effective management is particularly severe: an immense population suffers from PM2.5-related respiratory and cardiovascular conditions, with particulate matter contributing to over 1.42 million premature deaths annually and ranking as the fourth leading risk factor for mortality nationwide 5 , 6 . Although China maintains over 1700 national-level reference stations and thousands of provincial nodes, monitoring resources and analytical capacity remain unevenly distributed across regions 7 . This disparity creates a two-sided bottleneck in air quality management. In developed coastal agglomerations, the sheer volume and heterogeneity of monitoring data overwhelm existing expert capacity, resulting in analytical saturation. In western inland regions, by contrast, a shortage of skilled personnel and advanced analytical equipment means that even basic pollution analysis is frequently delayed, despite the availability of the underlying data 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 . Consequently, there is an urgent demand for an automated air pollution management system capable of performing complex analyses, presenting real-time data, and communicating with non-professional users. Such a system would provide critical support for both high-level decision-making and preliminary healthcare guidance, operating with less human intervention during the analytical phase, while preserving expert oversight for final policy decisions. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated emergent reasoning capabilities 12 , 13 , 14 , motivating a gradual shift from passive text-processing instruments toward increasingly autonomous scientific assistants 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 . However, the deployment of monolithic LLMs in air pollution research is fundamentally constrained. Even in expert-level environmental science and engineering question answering, GPT-4 attains an expert-rated factuality of only 0.79, dropping below 0.5 on the most difficult items 20 . This unreliability becomes critical in autonomous scientific workflows, where agentic systems succeed precisely because generated programs are filtered by external execution and evaluation rather than trusted 21 , 22 . This reliability gap widens in complex technical domains, where mainstream models frequently fail to surpass 50% accuracy in intricate calculations or domain-specific modeling, which is a limitation that fine-tuning for environmental applications has not fully resolved 20 , 23 . Furthermore, while fine-tuning embeds specific domain knowledge, it often precipitates narrow specialization and overfitting 20 , 24 , precluding the interdisciplinary breadth necessary for holistic urban air pollution analysis. More critically, LLMs possess a fundamental architectural deficit regarding complex numerical analysis 25 , 26 , 27 . These limitations are exacerbated by the challenge of integrating heterogeneous, high-frequency data streams, which frequently induces context loss and catastrophic forgetting in information-dense environments. Yet, despite their inherent lack of numerical competence, LLMs have achieved strong proficiency in code generation, enabling the synthesis of executable algorithmic scripts from natural language prompts 28 , 29 , 30 . Our proposed framework directly builds upon this capability. In this work, we introduce Neuro-Air, a multi-agent framework designed to overcome these structural limitations by automating core procedures within the urban air pollution management cycle. Rather than proposing a new retrieval or execution algorithm, the architecture integrates two capabilities from our prior systems, domain-grounded knowledge retrieval and a persistent code-execution runtime, within a hierarchical multi-agent workflow, so that the analytical pipeline is decomposed across specialized agents instead of being handled by a single monolithic model 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 . To our knowledge, this is among the first end-to-end analytical workflows of this kind assembled for air pollution analysis. Unlike prior approaches in environmental engineering that address narrowly defined analytical tasks 24 , 35 , 36 , 37 , Neuro-Air encompasses the full spectrum of the management cycle. It integrates real-time air quality data, screens emission source-receptor associations, evaluates multi-dimensional impacts, and synthesizes candidate control strategies. We evaluated the framework using a dataset of 317 complex environmental queries drawn from two contrasting application settings: acute emergency response in Hong Kong SAR, a densely monitored coastal metropolis, and chronic industrial-emission analysis in Hebei, a heavily industrialized region within the well-instrumented Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) air-quality coordination network. This validation utilized a rigorous human-in-the-loop protocol, cross-validating human expert assessments with autonomous AI Judges. To ensure methodological robustness, the framework was deployed across multiple mainstream LLM backbones to benchmark operational stability and consistency. Our results indicate that Neuro-Air mitigates several constraints of monolithic models on these tasks and provides a practical, reusable workflow for AI-assisted environmental analysis. The primary contributions of this work are threefold: Real-time assimilation of heterogeneous data: We establish an architectural protocol that integrates real-time, multimodal environmental data streams with semantic reasoning, designed to bridge the gap between raw sensor data and higher-level environmental analysis. Augmentation of LLM computational capacity: We augment foundation LLMs with algorithmic agents to mitigate their limitations in numerical reasoning and computation. This integration provides a foundation for executing complex atmospheric tasks, such as dynamic feature correlation and spatiotemporal analysis, that reside beyond the scope of monolithic models. Automation of interdisciplinary analytical workflows: We show that multi-agent collaboration can automate interdisciplinary analytical workflows that traditionally necessitate extensive human coordination, shortening the initial analytical synthesis and providing a scalable architecture to support environmental decision workflows. Ultimately, the framework serves as a decision-support tool for air quality management, with experts retaining responsibility for final judgments. It streamlines complex data integration and analytical workflows, thereby facilitating timely, data-driven regulatory action and public health protection. Results Functional overview and agent specialization A comprehensive urban air quality management lifecycle typically encompasses five core stages 38 : (1) objective identification, wherein governing institutions define policy thresholds, regulatory standards, and scientific baselines (e.g., acceptable pollutant concentrations or ozone formation mechanisms) through extensive literature and policy review; (2) pollution monitoring, which involves acquiring and processing high-resolution spatiotemporal data from sensor networks and satellite observations to establish early-warning systems and emergency analyses; (3) comprehensive assessment, requiring complex analytical procedures such as spatiotemporal distribution mapping, emission source-receptor association, meteorological factor analysis, and health risk evaluations; (4) strategy formulation, which translates complex analytical outputs into actionable mitigation strategies and accessible reports for non-expert stakeholders; and (5) continuous evaluation, ensuring ongoing monitoring of policy implementation and efficacy. To deploy LLMs across these interdisciplinary workflows, we built a suite of five domain-specific agents, structured into three functional modules: (i) External Knowledge and Data Extraction Agents (data pre-processing and knowledge retrieval); (ii) Analytical Agents (air pollution modeling and multi-variate analysis); and (iii) Synthesis and Communication Agents (visualization and report generation), as illustrated in Fig. 1 a. We used Claude 4 Sonnet 39 as the baseline backbone and benchmarked additional models (GPT-5 40 , Gemini 2.5 Pro 41 , DeepSeek-V3 42 ) in the evaluation below; each agent was initialized with a role-specific system prompt (shown in Table 2 ). This work builds upon two of our previous studies. The first is AirGPT 46 , a domain-specific framework that integrates conversational AI with atmospheric science expertise. By drawing on a curated corpus of peer-reviewed literature and specialized data analysis tools, AirGPT addresses the outdated and fabricated information that limits general-purpose LLMs in air quality management. The second is CaveAgent 47 , which employs a dual-stream architecture in which a persistent Python runtime, rather than the text context, serves as the central locus of state. Within minutes of the event, the isolated execution environment flagged acute short-term anomalies at the nearest monitoring station during the first 24 h: peak PM 2.5 reached 54.6 μ g/m 3 (2.0 × the pre-fire mean) and peak NO 2 reached 123.2 μ g/m 3 (2.5 × the pre-fire mean). The peak-to-mean ratio indicates the prominence of a short-term excursion but cannot, on its own, establish its cause, as similar peaks may arise from ordinary diurnal variability. The agent further cross-referenced the meteorological data and correctly noted that, despite the acute 24 h excursion, multi-day average pollution levels showed no sustained increase. Indeed, the station’s official AQHI remained within the moderate range (5–6) throughout the event. Because the screening signal alone does not support causal attribution, we conducted the follow-up analyses specified by our governance framework: comparison against a matched historical baseline, a before-and-after network contrast using a difference-in-differences framework, and an interrupted-time-series-style analysis of the event-aligned hourly observations. First, a historical matched-hour baseline (the same station and clock hour over the preceding 14 days) shows the fire-evening NO 2 value (123.2 μ g/m 3 at 20:00) to be high but not unprecedented ( z = 1.9; the matched-hour maximum of the preceding fortnight was 125.5 μ g/m 3 on a non-fire evening), confirming that the raw peak-to-mean ratio overstates the anomaly. Second, a network contrast across all 18 general and roadside stations, in which the nearest station’s change against its own matched-hour baseline is differenced against the contemporaneous changes at control stations (a difference-in-differences reading), reveals that the same evening exhibited a territory-wide NO 2 elevation (mean rise of + 19.6 μ g/m 3 against matched hours; several stations far upwind of the fire rose as much as, or more than, the nearest station), coincident with hour-resolved observations of the northeast-monsoon flow weakening to near calm in the sheltered Tolo basin during the evening hours–conditions favouring general pollutant accumulation rather than a single-source plume. Third, the pollutant contrast most consistent with a local combustion contribution tells the most defensible story: in the evening network contrast the nearest station ranked first of 18 in PM 2.5 elevation ( + 7.4 μ g/m 3 above the network mean rise), a modest but direction-consistent local excess. The resulting synthesis is appropriately qualified yet operationally useful. The expert reanalysis indicates that the fire’s discernible local signature was a moderate elevation in particulate matter superimposed on a broader regional stagnation episode. The system’s minutes-scale screening appropriately prompted further investigation, whereas final attribution depended on expert-level reanalysis. This delineation of responsibilities reflects the human-AI division of labour underlying our framework. As part of the real-time workflow, the system complemented pollutant screening with a proximity-based indicator of potential exposure. It generated four concentric distance bands (0–500, 500–1000, 1000–1500, and 1500–2000 m) around the incident location and identified 293 residential buildings within 1.5 km, as well as 1090 buildings of all types within 2 km. The scope of interpretation, however, should be clearly delineated. In the absence of plume-direction, occupancy, and population data, these building counts indicate spatial proximity to the incident but do not quantify population exposure. Nevertheless, the analysis identified the Tai Po Baptist Public School, located approximately 35 m from the incident location, as a potentially sensitive receptor warranting priority expert review and consideration of targeted health communication. It then used the LLM’s semantic reasoning capabilities to translate the quantitative results into an accessible natural-language report for non-specialist stakeholders. Second, the system grounds its analytical outputs in retrieved data and reproducible computations. Its visualizations, recommendations, and advisories are therefore traceable to the underlying evidence and analytical procedures, allowing experts to inspect and verify each stage of the analytical process. Multi-variate emission analysis Beyond rapid emergency response, environmental management requires multi-variate analysis of the non-linear interactions among meteorology, traffic, and static geographical features such as topography and land use. To demonstrate this, we present an exploratory source-receptor association and emission-concentration screening case study of an iron smelting facility in Handan, Hebei (Fig. 4 ). Handan is a heavily industrialized priority city within the BTH air-quality coordination network. Its local analytical capacity, however, remains constrained by limited specialized expertise and advanced instrumentation, such that even routine air-quality assessments can require considerable time and manual effort. This setting complements the acute-event Hong Kong case by demonstrating the potential value of the framework in contexts with limited analytical resources. In this scenario, Neuro-Air is tasked with analyzing carbon monoxide (CO) emissions from a large iron and steel facility in She County, Handan, Hebei, and evaluating their localized impact on ambient air quality from January 6 to 8, 2026 (Fig. 4 a). Upon receiving the query, the Central Coordinator orchestrates the analytical pipeline. Initially, the knowledge retrieval and data acquisition agents identify the facility’s spatial coordinates and extract the corresponding high-resolution emission logs and operational data. To identify which nearby monitoring stations are most strongly associated with the facility’s emissions, Neuro-Air autonomously geofences candidate receptor stations around the source and screens them by emission-concentration cross-correlation, rather than a physically calibrated dispersion model. By setting a 20 km geofencing radius, the agent retrieves synchronized ambient monitoring data from 18 surrounding stations. It then generates native Python code leveraging the SciPy library to compute the cross-correlation between the facility’s emission vectors and the ambient concentration signals (Fig. 4 b). Through this isolated execution, the system ranks the candidate receptors by association strength, identifying the Guxin Air Quality Station (located 12.2 km to the south of the facility) as the most strongly associated node, with a strong baseline correlation ( r = 0.77, p < 0.01). Algorithm 2 Autonomous source-receptor Association and Time-Lag Correlation To characterize the temporal association between facility emissions and ambient pollutant concentrations, the system calculated Pearson correlations over candidate lags of τ ∈ {0, …, 6} h. At Guxin, the maximum correlation occurred when concentrations were aligned with emissions recorded three hours earlier ( r = 0.83; Fig. 4 c). We interpret this as an empirical lag of maximum correlation, not a physically resolved transport time. Following Algorithm 2, the system then defined high- and low-emission periods using the 67th and 33rd percentiles of the emission distribution ( > 14, 288 and < 11, 857 kg/h, respectively). Under contemporaneous alignment, mean CO at Guxin was approximately 130% higher during high-emission hours than during low-emission hours (0.78 versus 0.34 mg/m 3 ), with a corresponding 16-point difference in mean AQI (Fig. 4 d). Because this comparison did not incorporate the identified three-hour lag, we repeated the analysis using lag-aligned observations. The association remained similar or slightly stronger: under the original thresholds, the difference in mean CO between high- and low-emission periods was + 0.46 mg/m 3 ( + 139%), accompanied by an 18-point difference in mean AQI. After recalculating the thresholds using the 69 lag-aligned pairs, the relative difference was + 134%. A moving-block bootstrap using 12-hour blocks and B = 10 4 replicates, with thresholds re-estimated in each replicate, yielded 95% intervals that excluded zero under both contemporaneous alignment ( + 0.20 to + 0.59 mg/m 3 ) and three-hour lag alignment ( + 0.24 to + 0.57 mg/m 3 ). The intervals remained bounded away from zero for block lengths of 6–24 h, indicating that the original difference between emission periods was not an artifact of temporal alignment. Nevertheless, these results represent statistical associations rather than causal effects and remain subject to the confounding factors discussed below. The system also generated targeted visualizations and a management-oriented synthesis (Fig. 4 e); the complete workflow and original response are provided in Supplementary Note 4 . Algorithm 2 summarizes the lag-consistent screening procedure used by Neuro-Air: it retrieves facility emission records and concentration measurements from nearby monitoring stations, evaluates candidate lags, identifies the lag of maximum correlation, retains associations that meet the predefined screening criterion, and compares concentrations between high- and low-emission periods under the selected temporal alignment. This analysis supports a statistical association between facility emissions and concentrations measured at Guxin, not a physically validated attribution of the observed pollution to the facility. Because the system’s meteorological query returned no wind records for the analysis window, its directional labels reflected only the geometric relationship between the facility and monitoring station; the wind field was therefore reconstructed independently by experts. Predominantly southwesterly winds prevailed and generally did not support direct transport from the facility to Guxin, which is located to its south. Shared diurnal cycles, regional background pollution, or emissions from other nearby facilities may therefore partly explain the observed correlation. Robust attribution is further limited by the short record ( ~ 72 hourly observations), temporal autocorrelation, shared meteorological forcing, and selection of the maximum correlation across 18 monitoring stations and 7 candidate lags. The r > 0.5 and p < 0.01 thresholds in Algorithm 2 should therefore be treated as descriptive screening criteria rather than formal inferential tests, particularly because the original procedure did not correct for the 126 comparisons arising from all combinations of stations and lags. As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, a Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all 126 comparisons retained the association between Guxin concentrations and facility emissions at a three-hour lag at a nominal false-discovery rate of 0.01 (unadjusted p = 7.3 × 10 −19 ). This correction, however, does not account for temporal dependence or environmental confounding. Moreover, 48 combinations involving 9 of the 18 monitoring stations met the original screening criteria, indicating a spatially diffuse pattern of correlations. This pattern is consistent with shared diurnal variation and regional background pollution rather than an association uniquely localized to Guxin. An independent companion analysis likewise did not support a physically credible transport relationship: the correlation was higher under winds unfavourable for transport from the facility to Guxin ( r = 0.777, n = 62) than under favourable winds ( r = 0.640, n = 8), although the small number of favourable-wind observations limits this comparison. The inconsistent physical interpretations obtained from the same data demonstrate that reliable code execution does not ensure stable source attribution and underscore the need for expert review. Regulatory use would require wind-resolved dispersion analysis, correction for multiple comparisons across monitoring stations and candidate lags, explicit treatment of temporal dependence and confounding, and validation using independent observations. A key factor underlying Neuro-Air’s effectiveness in this workflow is its dual-stream architecture, which decouples semantic reasoning from mathematical computation. General-purpose LLMs may produce unsupported outputs in pollution source attribution, such as selecting monitoring stations without sufficient scientific justification, and such limitations cannot be fully resolved by simply embedding physical equations in the prompt. Conversely, task-specific fine-tuned models may lack the flexibility required for changing analytical conditions. Neuro-Air addresses these limitations by using the LLM for logical orchestration and code generation while delegating numerical modeling to an isolated Python environment. This separation improves methodological transparency by allowing researchers to inspect generated scripts, intermediate outputs, and data logs for analytical validity and data provenance. For complex atmospheric analyses involving iterative and multi-step calculations, direct Python execution can also reduce repeated model calls and external tool interactions, thereby lowering latency, computational overhead, and token consumption. By transforming heterogeneous sensor data into actionable information, Neuro-Air bridges raw environmental observations and downstream decision-making, providing preliminary guidance on pollution conditions and associated health considerations. Evaluation of Neuro-Air’s performance We assembled a multi-source dataset (air quality, traffic, meteorological, geospatial, and emission data) for Hong Kong 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 and Hebei Province, and curated a benchmark of 317 representative queries (200 Hebei, 117 Hong Kong) drawn from practitioner interviews, official government documentation, and citizen inquiries 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 ; the two queries driving the case studies are held out and reported separately. The queries are stratified into six functional domains (Temporal Pattern, Geospatial, Multivariate Environmental, Urban Environmental Planning, Environmental Decision Support, and Personal Environmental Action Planning), collectively spanning spatio-temporal analysis, city-level planning, and management guidance. Full metadata and the benchmark inventory are in Supplementary Note 1 (Data Acquisition and Benchmark Construction), which lists the query inventories for Hebei and Hong Kong (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 ) alongside the corresponding data sources and characteristics (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 ). To rigorously assess Neuro-Air and mitigate hallucinations across the entire analytical lifecycle, we score every response against five core criteria: (1) Data Integration and Synthesis (DIS), which assesses the absolute accuracy of empirical data retrieval; (2) Methodological Soundness (MS), which evaluates the contextual appropriateness of the selected analytical models; (3) Technical Implementation Quality (TIQ), which verifies the executable validity of the generated programmatic code; (4) Response Alignment (RA), which measures the comprehensive fulfillment of the user’s initial query; and (5) Faithfulness (FAI), which ensures that all final conclusions are strictly derived from transparent, intermediate computational evidence. The comprehensive performance score is the average of these five criteria. Detailed operational definitions and step-by-step verification protocols are provided in Supplementary Note 2 (Detailed Evaluation Framework and Protocols); the overall 1–10 scoring scale is given in Supplementary Table 5 , and the complete scoring rubric applied by the human experts is provided in Supplementary Note 5 , with the full scale as administered to the panel in Supplementary Table 11 . We apply these criteria through four complementary channels 46 , 59 , 60 , organized around two levels of quality. Execution-level quality, namely data retrieval, computation, and code execution, is captured by the three automated channels; professional-level quality, namely whether a response is dependable enough to inform a real-world air-quality management decision and thus requires environmental judgment that execution accuracy alone cannot capture, is probed by the human panel. (i) A multiple AI-judge evaluation scores all 317 queries on the five criteria using three independent LLM judges (Kimi-K2, Claude 4 Sonnet, Qwen3), each judge scoring every response once, with the final score taken as the mean across the three judges, across the four foundation-model backbones. (ii) A judge-free objective channel grades 90 programmatically verified tasks against ground truth recomputed live from the database (this benchmark is detailed below with the ablation). (iii) Objective process metrics are parsed deterministically from the complete execution logs. (iv) A blinded human calibration study was conducted with 15 active environmental-management professionals, including atmospheric analysts, data engineers, and policymakers. Each evaluator independently scored the responses generated by the baseline Claude 4 Sonnet backbone under the full multi-agent configuration using a holistic 1–10 rubric. Evaluation was performed on a difficulty-weighted subset of 32 queries (11 from Hong Kong and 21 from Hebei) covering all six task categories. The subset was selected by stratified random sampling across task categories, followed by manual review to retain more challenging items. In the fully crossed design, evaluators could inspect the generated code and intermediate execution outputs alongside the final answer, but were blinded to the automated scores; disjoint calibration examples were provided for reference. Because standalone LLM judges can exhibit internal biases 61 , 62 , 63 , the human panel and the judge-free channels serve as independent cross-checks. Full panel composition, recruitment, consent, protocols, rubric, and statistics are provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Methods. The four evaluation backbones (GPT-5, Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and DeepSeek-V3) were accessed under standardized conditions during August 2025 (sampling temperature 0.2, provider default for GPT-5): Claude 4 Sonnet (snapshot claude-sonnet-4-20250514 ) and DeepSeek-V3 (V3-0324) via their official APIs, and GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro through an OpenAI-compatible gateway. The process-metrics log analysis additionally covers Qwen3-235B-A22B and Kimi-K2 (six backbones in total), whereas the architecture ablation below uses a separate set of five more recent backbones (Methods): because the architecture is backbone-agnostic and the available models advanced during the revision, Neuro-Air supports drop-in replacement of updated backbones without modifying the agent workflow. The full set of backbones and judges, and the roles they play, is summarized in Supplementary Table 9 . Finally, we isolate two design choices through matched ablations, varying one factor at a time while keeping the backbone, database, persistent Python runtime, system prompt, execution-step budget, and number of attempts fixed (Methods). The shared step budget limits the number of code executions in each arm, but does not enforce equal overall compute; the additional token cost of the multi-agent configuration is reported separately below. Both ablations are evaluated on a 90-task programmatically verified (PV) subset, for which outputs can be deterministically recomputed from the source data and assessed by an automatic validator without human or LLM-based judging (Fig. 5 ; Methods). For the architecture ablation, we compare the full multi-agent topology with a single-agent configuration in which all roles are merged. Because the worker roles form sequentially dependent stages of the workflow, removing an individual worker would disrupt the pipeline rather than provide a meaningful partial ablation; we therefore use the fully merged configuration as the comparison. For the execution-paradigm ablation, we compare native Python execution with a conventional function-calling workflow. Function calling requires repeated exchanges between the model and external tools during iterative analyses, whereas Neuro-Air generates the analytical procedure as executable Python code and runs it within an isolated sandbox. This reduces repeated communication and token use while keeping numerical operations within a reproducible execution environment. We therefore conducted a blinded expert-panel evaluation to provide a more demanding assessment of professional quality. Automated judges consistently assigned higher scores than the experts, with a mean difference of + 0.24 on the 0–1 scale across task types (Fig. 6 b). This individual-level disagreement highlights the inherent subjectivity of professional judgments on complex analytical outputs. The systematic score gap suggests that experts applied a stricter standard of professional adequacy. This was particularly evident for health-impact questions and source-attribution tasks. For the latter, professional assessment often requires domain-grounded emission characterization and dispersion modeling, whereas Neuro-Air typically relied on proximity- or correlation-based reasoning. These results illustrate that correct execution does not necessarily translate into professionally adequate analysis. Full agreement and inter-rater reliability statistics are reported in Supplementary Note 2 . The gap was driven by a recurring set of failure modes that became more frequent as task complexity increased. On questions requiring source-receptor or transport reasoning, the system frequently substituted same-hour concentration correlation or simple proximity for genuine dispersion or trajectory analysis, omitting wind-direction gating, transport lag, and plume decay, and in some cases identified a facility that was in fact downwind as a dominant contributor. Temporal and causal mismatches arose when long-term or multi-day averages, combined with small-sample correlations, were used to answer questions about specific dates or causal attribution. The system also did not consistently validate query premises against the retrieved data: it sometimes accepted a stated “strong wind” when measurements indicated light winds, or a “severe PM 2.5 alert” when the exceedance concerned PM 10 and no alert was in effect, and some failed wind, gradient, or direction queries were dropped silently rather than surfaced. These failures concentrated in the more complex source-receptor, spatial-exposure, and health-assessment tasks, whereas straightforward temporal-comparison queries were handled most reliably; this gradient mirrors the objective difficulty pattern reported above and delimits where the current system can and cannot be trusted. In summary, the four evaluation channels converge on a consistent but carefully bounded picture. The automated multi-judge protocol and objective process metrics indicate that Neuro-Air’s execution core is highly reliable, with aggregate scores above 85%. The controlled ablation further shows that the complete multi-agent pipeline improves programmatically verified accuracy over a merged single-agent baseline across all tested backbones, with a ~ 3B-active-parameter backbone in the multi-agent configuration reaching the accuracy range of a much larger backbone operated as a single agent. The blinded expert evaluation, however, identifies important limits to autonomous use. Performance remains less dependable for source-attribution, spatial-exposure, and health-assessment tasks that require transport modeling, premise validation, or domain-specific professional judgment. More importantly, the health-related outputs produced by Neuro-Air are not based on clinical diagnosis or individualized medical assessment and therefore should not be treated as validated health advice or communicated directly to the public without appropriate professional review. These findings distinguish reliable computational execution from professional adequacy. Neuro-Air can rapidly integrate and analyze heterogeneous environmental data, but its outputs, particularly those involving health implications, should be regarded as decision-support information rather than a substitute for expert environmental, public-health, or clinical judgment. Discussion In this study, we introduce Neuro-Air, an autonomous, multi-agent framework engineered to serve as an end-to-end analytical assistant for urban air quality management. By automating the analytical lifecycle, the system performs fundamental air pollution management tasks, such as acute emergency early-warning and exploratory source-receptor association, and synthesizes non-expert visualizations for public communication. The architectural core is a dual-stream design, inherited from our CaveAgent runtime 47 , that decouples semantic reasoning from mathematical execution; the contribution here is its integration with domain-grounded retrieval under a hierarchical multi-agent workflow, which delivers auditable performance across multiple LLM backbones and task domains. Neuro-Air can therefore substantially shorten the initial analytical synthesis that traditionally required extended inter-institutional coordination, while incurring bounded, transparently reported computational cost and preserving the capacity for subsequent expert review. A matched ablation shows that the complete multi-agent pipeline outperforms a merged single-agent baseline under the same execution-step cap, albeit at 3.3–4.4 × the token cost, positioning Neuro-Air as a capability equalizer that delivers accurate quantitative performance from smaller, lower-cost backbones in resource-constrained governance settings. Crucially, this design also mitigates the transparency issues typically associated with LLMs: because the workflow generates explicit, readable code and data logs, environmental engineers and policymakers can directly audit the system’s reasoning, data provenance, and execution logic. Compared with previous domain-specific LLM applications, Neuro-Air is firmly anchored to the empirical realities of acute pollution management, prioritizing functional execution and computational accuracy over generalized text generation. The scope of this evidence is nonetheless bounded. Our results support Neuro-Air as a reusable analytical workflow rather than a single tuned demonstration; however, both study regions are data-rich settings with structured, well-maintained databases, and performance in data-scarce or differently structured environments remains untested. We therefore frame the contribution as a transferable methodology that can be re-instantiated for new regions and tasks, while emphasizing that broader external validation is required before strong claims of general applicability can be made. Within these bounds, we further characterized the system’s failure modes through blinded expert review of the complete responses, including the generated code and intermediate outputs. Although the system addresses straightforward temporal-comparison queries with high reliability, it still lacks the ability to perform certain operations required by complex tasks such as source-receptor or spatial-exposure problems, including wind-gated, transport-lagged reasoning, correct geospatial handling, and premise validation. For health assessment, another limitation applies: the system’s outputs are not based on clinical diagnosis or calibrated against professional medical or public-health standards, and should therefore be treated only as preliminary reference information rather than validated health advice. In these cases, expert review is essential to ensure that the conclusions drawn from the system’s analysis are valid and actionable. Overall, while Neuro-Air represents an advancement in AI-assisted environmental decision support, its limitations and the continued need for human oversight in complex environmental analyses must be recognized. Taken together, these results define the role of Neuro-Air more clearly. Under data-rich conditions, the system provides a dependable computational core for air-quality analysis. It can retrieve and integrate heterogeneous environmental data, including emission, meteorological, and geospatial information, and perform accurate, reproducible, and auditable code-based analyses. However, it is not yet a fully autonomous decision-making system for complex environmental questions. Neuro-Air does not perform mechanistic dispersion or trajectory modeling, nor does it provide regulatory-grade source apportionment. Its quantitative outputs are therefore better interpreted as associational and exploratory rather than causal. This distinction is particularly important for source-attribution, proximity-based exposure estimation, and analyses based on short observation windows, incomplete wind information, or potentially confounded correlations. In such settings, the system can identify patterns and generate screening signals for further investigation, but reliability decreases as task complexity, data limitations, and the need for transport reasoning increase. These limitations become more consequential when the outputs could inform policy or health-related decisions. Neuro-Air should not be used autonomously for professional-grade attribution, healthcare recommendations, or major emission-management decisions. In these settings, its outputs should serve as preliminary evidence for expert review rather than as a basis for direct action. Accordingly, the main value of Neuro-Air lies in streamlining the early stages of environmental analysis. It organizes relevant data, performs reproducible computation, and presents initial patterns and statistics in an accessible form, providing a foundation for more detailed and context-specific professional assessment. Building on these findings, a central near-term goal is the development of a more comprehensive air quality management benchmark that enables real-life analysis. Bridging LLM-based generative reasoning with deterministic atmospheric physical modeling remains a substantial challenge, and we intend to pursue directions that combine LLMs with expert environmental knowledge, such as mechanistic dispersion modeling, and potentially with established numerical models such as GEOS-Chem, WRF-Chem, and CMAQ. We hope that this exploration will establish a foundation for AI-assisted decision support in urban environmental management. Methods Data The environmental monitoring data utilized in this study were obtained from two distinct regions: Hong Kong and Hebei Province. For Hong Kong, air quality data were sourced from the Hong Kong Environmental Protection Department (EPD) 49 and the Common Spatial Data Infrastructure (CSDI) 50 . These datasets provide hourly pollutant concentrations (including PM 2.5 , PM 10 , SO 2 , NO 2 , O 3 , and CO) alongside official Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) records for all monitoring stations. Traffic data were derived from the Hong Kong Transport Department’s open data service, encompassing real-time vehicle speeds, road speed limits, and detailed road network geometry, which together enable the characterization of congestion states and transport dynamics 51 . Meteorological observations from the Hong Kong Observatory’s automatic weather station network were incorporated to capture atmospheric variables at a fine temporal resolution 52 . Additionally, point-of-interest and land-use information from OpenStreetMap (OSM) 53 and environmental spatial layers from the Hong Kong Environmental Data Hub (HKED) 54 were integrated to establish a detailed urban and ecological context. A comprehensive summary of the Hong Kong dataset is provided in Supplementary Table 3 . For Hebei Province, the dataset encompasses a hierarchical air quality network that monitors key pollutants at hourly and daily resolutions across station, district, and city levels. This is coupled with a detailed emission inventory covering point and area sources across six major industrial sectors, alongside concurrent meteorological parameters, high-resolution wind fields, and specialized construction dust tracking. All spatial data are standardized to the WGS84 coordinate system to integrate with urban land-use features. The raw environmental monitoring records for this region were obtained from the Department of Ecology and Environment of Hebei Province. Due to governmental data protection policies, these original data are proprietary, restricted, and not publicly available. However, processed and aggregated results supporting the findings of this study are included in Supplementary Table 4 . For verification purposes, qualified researchers may contact the corresponding author to request access to specific processed data subsets, subject to approval from the original data provider and compliance with data-sharing agreements. The anonymized expert-rating matrix (32 responses × 15 raters) and the per-response automated judge scores underlying the human-evaluation calibration are available from the corresponding author upon request. The programmatic-verification benchmark (task definitions and frozen validators), the deterministic scoring pipeline, and the agent and prompt configurations are publicly available at https://github.com/acodercat/Neuro-Air ; the raw Hebei environmental records remain subject to the data-provider restrictions described above. Functioning agents Neuro-Air integrates a suite of specialized environmental functional agents that collectively encompass a comprehensive range of analytical capabilities. These agents are systematically designed to address the multifaceted demands of urban air-pollution assessment and environmental data analytics. Functionally, they constitute the foundational blueprint that governs how the LLM interacts with external data sources, computational tools, and user instructions within an environmental-science context. By explicitly defining the agent’s identity, technical environment, operational workflow, and behavioral boundaries, Neuro-Air moves beyond the conventional role of conversational systems and operates as an analytical framework capable of querying structured databases, executing Python-based computations, and dynamically generating interpretable visualizations. Through detailed specifications of its functional components, such as the integrated database engine for real-time air-quality queries and the ECharts configuration manager for interactive data visualization, the system prompt bridges natural-language reasoning with scientific-grade computation. This structured architecture ensures that Neuro-Air’s responses remain contextually precise, methodologically rigorous, and empirically verifiable. The internal logic of Neuro-Air’s architecture is organized around three interdependent layers: (i) a role-definition layer, in which Neuro-Air is conceptualized as a multidisciplinary environmental intelligence framework capable of interpreting, analyzing, and visualizing complex air-pollution phenomena. It merges the interpretative flexibility of large language models with the procedural rigor of environmental informatics and data science. (ii) a task-execution layer, whereby the system prompt formalizes the entire analytical workflow by which natural-language queries are transformed into executable procedures. This encompasses structured data retrieval through SQL, computational reasoning in Python, spatiotemporal aggregation, and visualization synthesis. Each process follows a precisely defined procedural syntax, ensuring analytical reproducibility and cross-session consistency; and (iii) a constraint-and-compliance layer: to safeguard analytical integrity, the framework enforces strict methodological constraints: Neuro-Air is restricted to tasks relevant to the air-quality database, must seek clarification when confronted with ambiguous queries, and is required to produce concise Python code encapsulated within a standardized <think> + code block format. External tools or unverified resources are disallowed. This constraint-driven design is intended to support transparency, accuracy, and auditability across all analyses. Collectively, these elements constitute a coherent cognitive and operational framework that establishes Neuro-Air as a new class of LLM-driven scientific reasoning systems, bridging natural-language interaction with quantitative environmental intelligence. First, we established a human-reviewed reference set to verify data retrieval and numerical computation. A subset of complex SQL and Python queries was manually executed against the curated environmental databases, including municipal sensor networks and meteorological records. The resulting outputs were used as deterministic references for evaluating whether the multi-agent system retrieved the correct source data and reproduced the corresponding numerical results. This procedure was intended to distinguish empirically grounded outputs from unsupported or fabricated numerical claims. Second, during system development, we tested the agents with deliberately invalid or underspecified queries to examine whether they would recognize unsupported premises rather than infer missing information. These probes included spatial traps, such as requests involving nonexistent or decommissioned monitoring stations; temporal traps, such as requests for observational data from future dates; chemical traps, such as treating secondary pollutants including ozone as directly emitted primary pollutants; and queries lacking essential spatial or temporal constraints. For these cases, an appropriate response was defined as identifying the invalid premise, declining to proceed, or requesting the missing information. These development-time probes informed the design of the relevance gate and execution guardrails, which are intended to prevent downstream analysis when the required premises or inputs are not sufficiently supported. Third, we assessed the faithfulness of the final narrative outputs using an ensemble of independent LLM evaluators. The Faithfulness metric evaluates whether claims in the final response are supported by the system’s executed computational evidence. Evaluators were provided with the final analytical response together with the relevant intermediate computation traces and code outputs, and assessed whether numerical claims, statistical summaries, and analytical conclusions could be traced to executed results. Claims lacking identifiable computational support were penalized under this criterion. Finally, we evaluated the executable validity of the SQL and Python code generated by the specialized worker agents. Technical Implementation Quality was assessed with an emphasis on first-attempt execution, including syntax validity, consistency with database schemas, valid library usage, and absence of runtime exceptions. Initial execution failures were penalized even if later corrected, allowing the metric to capture the robustness of autonomously generated code before iterative recovery. This criterion therefore evaluates whether the generated analytical procedures are not only logically appropriate but also operationally executable when first issued. Matched single-agent versus multi-agent ablation To evaluate the contribution of the multi-agent architecture independently of subjective judging, we constructed a 90-task programmatically verified (PV) subset from the 317-query benchmark (Fig. 5 ). The subset comprised 78 Hebei and 12 Hong Kong queries and covered five task categories: spatial analysis (27), data retrieval (20), comparative assessment (18), statistical characterization (16), and temporal pattern analysis (9). Tasks were eligible only when the expected output could be expressed as one or more uniquely determined numeric or textual values that were recomputable from the source databases. Open-ended synthesis, qualitative ranking, and policy or action-planning tasks were therefore excluded. The subset was curated before model evaluation and frozen for use across all experimental conditions. Ground-truth values were generated using human-reviewed SQL and Python reference implementations. At scoring time, each validator re-executed the corresponding reference computation against the source database and compared the result with typed variables produced by the agent. Numeric outputs were assessed using predefined task-specific tolerances, whereas counts and identifiers required exact matches. The validator set was frozen before the experimental campaign, hidden from the agents, and applied identically to both architectures. Final scoring was performed against an idle database to avoid database contention being misclassified as task failure. Scoring was therefore deterministic and involved neither human judgment nor an LLM judge. The single-agent condition merged all specialized roles into one agent with access to the complete toolchain. The multi-agent condition used the Central Coordinator, five specialized worker agents, and the in-loop computational auditing stage, with all agents sharing a persistent Python runtime. For each backbone, the two conditions used the same database, runtime environment, sampling temperature, and a byte-identical system prompt verified by content fingerprint. Both conditions were limited to 20 execution steps and a single attempt per task; in the multi-agent condition, the 20-step budget was shared across agents. This constraint equalized the permitted number of execution steps rather than token consumption, with token costs reported separately in Supplementary Table 6 . No backbone-specific prompt tuning was performed. Five backbones spanning different model families and capability levels were evaluated: DeepSeek-V4-Flash, GPT-5.5, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen2.5-72B, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B. Sampling temperature was held identical between architectural conditions for each backbone, subject to provider API constraints. Because provider APIs did not expose sampling seeds, run-level reproducibility was bounded by the fixed sampling configuration, documented model snapshots and access windows, and the single-attempt protocol. All completed runs were archived in full. Provider-side endpoint failures were defined as runs recording zero execution steps, zero tokens, and an empty variable set. These infrastructure failures were excluded symmetrically and re-run until each architectural condition contained 90 genuinely executed tasks per backbone. Architecture effects were then assessed using exact two-sided McNemar tests on paired task outcomes for each backbone and across the 450 pooled task-backbone pairs. Pooled accuracy estimates are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Because the same 90 tasks were reused across all five backbones, we additionally performed a task-level cluster bootstrap in which tasks were resampled with replacement while retaining their five paired backbone observations ( B = 100, 000, seed 20260709). Full statistical specifications, exclusion criteria, and execution-cost metrics are reported in Supplementary Table 6 . Execution-paradigm comparison To compare execution paradigms, we evaluated a function-calling baseline in which the agent accessed the same Hong Kong databases through discrete tool calls, with tool outputs returned to the model as structured messages. This differs from Neuro-Air’s native-execution approach, in which analytical logic is generated as code and executed within a persistent Python runtime. The comparison included 117 function-calling runs collected in August 2025 and 702 Hong Kong runs from the six backbones evaluated under native execution. Logs from both settings were analyzed using the same rule-based parser and metric definitions, including first-attempt execution success, the proportion of runs containing execution errors, and the distribution of errors at the SQL or tool interface. Because the function-calling baseline predates the standardized experimental protocol and its backbone identity was not retained, this comparison is not a fully matched factorial experiment. We therefore use it only to characterize execution-level differences between the two paradigms and do not draw backbone-specific conclusions. Technical design Neuro-Air supports urban air-quality analyses across a range of complexity levels. For clarity, we group these tasks into three broad categories: basic queries involving information retrieval and descriptive statistics; intermediate queries requiring data aggregation, feature extraction, visualization, or multivariate analysis; and advanced queries involving multi-stage environmental assessment and decision support. These categories are descriptive rather than rigid, as a single workflow may involve operations from multiple levels. For basic retrieval tasks, the system first interprets the user query q and evaluates whether the requested information is supported by the available data sources. Let \({\mathcal{D}}\) denote the available database schema. A relevance function \(R(q,{\mathcal{D}})\) determines whether the query is sufficiently aligned with the available data: $$R(q,{\mathcal{D}})={\mathbb{I}}\,\left[{\rm{sim}}(q,{\mathcal{D}})\ge \tau \right],$$ (1) where \({\rm{sim}}(\cdot ,\cdot )\) denotes semantic similarity and θ is the relevance threshold ( θ = 0.5 in our implementation). Queries that do not satisfy this criterion are halted or returned for clarification rather than being processed under unsupported assumptions. When external contextual information is required, the knowledge-retrieval component \({\mathcal{K}}\) retrieves relevant information from approved external sources. For intermediate and advanced analyses, Neuro-Air uses a dual-stream architecture that separates semantic reasoning from numerical execution. At interaction step t , the system maintains a semantic state $${H}_{t}={h}_{t},{o}_{t},$$ (2) where h t contains the reasoning history and o t contains observations returned by the execution environment, together with a persistent runtime state $${S}_{t}={N}_{t},{R}_{t},$$ (3) where N t contains active variables, functions, models, and data objects, and R t contains intermediate computational results. The Semantic Stream generates computational instructions from the current query and semantic context: $${c}_{t}={f}_{\theta }({h}_{t},{q}_{t}),$$ (4) where f θ denotes the underlying language model. The Runtime Stream executes these instructions within the persistent Python environment: $${S}_{t+1}={\Phi }({S}_{t},{c}_{t}),$$ (5) where Φ( ⋅ ) represents the runtime execution operator. The persistent namespace retains previously generated computational objects across successive steps, $${N}_{t+1}\supseteq {N}_{t},$$ (6) allowing multi-step analyses to proceed without repeatedly reconstructing intermediate data or computational state. Complex workflows are decomposed into an ordered sequence of analytical tasks, $${\mathcal{W}}={T}_{1},{T}_{2},\ldots ,{T}_{n},$$ (7) which may include data preprocessing, statistical analysis, geospatial processing, visualization, and environmental assessment. Each stage operates on the outputs of preceding stages: $${R}_{i+1}={{\Phi }}_{i}({R}_{i},{c}_{i}),$$ (8) where Φ i ( ⋅ ) denotes the execution operator associated with task T i . The resulting intermediate outputs are subsequently integrated into a structured analytical response containing relevant quantitative results, visualizations, and contextual interpretation. This separation between semantic orchestration and persistent numerical execution allows Neuro-Air to maintain computational continuity across multi-step workflows while keeping the generated analytical procedures inspectable and reproducible. Operational safety and reliability Because Neuro-Air is intended for decision-support applications, its execution environment incorporates safeguards for data integrity, code execution, untrusted input, and failure containment. All agents access the environmental databases, including sensor networks, emission inventories, and meteorological records, through read-only connections. The system therefore cannot insert, modify, or delete source records during analysis. Generated Python and SQL code is executed within an isolated runtime separated from the semantic reasoning process (see Technical Design). Before execution, a static security checker inherited from the CaveAgent runtime 47 screens each code block for prohibited operations. These include database-mutating statements such as UPDATE , DROP , and DELETE ; interpreter-escape or process-control operations; disallowed imports; and other non-whitelisted system operations. Code that fails these checks is rejected before execution. Neuro-Air also separates trusted system and role instructions from untrusted user input and externally retrieved content. Retrieved text is treated as data rather than executable instruction, while relevance filtering limits the external information admitted into the analytical workflow. This separation is intended to reduce the risk of prompt injection or instruction redirection from untrusted content. A dedicated adversarial security evaluation was not conducted in this study. To constrain resource use and prevent uncontrolled execution, each query is subject to a fixed execution-step budget, bounded retry and auto-correction attempts, and predefined limits on spatial and temporal query scope. These constraints prevent unbounded execution loops and support reproducible evaluation of first-attempt code validity. For auditability, the system records the generated SQL and Python code, execution outputs, intermediate variables, and retrieved inputs for each task. Quantitative claims in the final response can therefore be compared against the corresponding computational traces, forming the basis of the Faithfulness evaluation described above. When required data are unavailable or a query contains an invalid premise, the system is designed to halt execution, request clarification, or return the limitation rather than infer unsupported values. These safeguards support the use of Neuro-Air as an analytical decision-support system, while professional review remains necessary before its outputs are used for consequential policy, operational, or health-related decisions.
